Great questions. I’d frame the overall answer like this:

> Your work is novel if the claim is not “we invented gradient circuit tracing,” but “we shift circuit discovery from human-defined output behaviours to model-internal latent dependencies, and introduce seed-latent-centered counterfactual/ablation/hybrid discovery methods for mapping those dependencies.”

That is a real research angle.

## 1. If The Papers Build On Prior Work, Can We Still Be Novel?

Yes. Novelty in this area is usually **not** “no one has ever used gradients/SAEs/patching before.” SFC, Circuit Tracing, and ADAG all build on attribution patching, activation patching, SAEs/transcoders, causal mediation, feature visualization, etc.

Their novelty is in **composition and framing**:

- SFC: sparse feature circuits plus editability.
- Circuit Tracing: replacement/local replacement model plus attribution graphs.
- ADAG: automated description of attribution graphs.

Your possible novelty is similarly compositional:

- moving the endpoint from logits/behaviour to **latent-to-latent dependency**;
- treating latents as model-internal computational variables worth explaining directly;
- distinguishing **counterfactual activators**, **inhibitors**, and **positive-context supports**;
- evaluating whether latent interventions recover or suppress another latent.

So yes: by the same standard, your approach can be novel. The defensible claim is **not foundational novelty**, but **a new target, objective, and evaluation setup for SAE-feature dependency discovery**.

## 2. Your Main Proposal Is Strong

I think your argument is good:

> Outputs are filtered summaries of computation. They are not the whole computation.

Logit/output-centred interpretability is powerful because it gives a clear task metric, but it is also bottlenecked by human task definitions. A model computes many internal variables that may never be cleanly exposed as a single behaviour. SAE latents are imperfect, but they are one way to sample the model’s internal ontology.

So your research direction could be stated as:

> Instead of only asking which features caused an output, we ask which features cause other features, building a dependency graph over the model’s own learned variables.

That is scientifically interesting because it shifts the unit of explanation from:

> human-labelled task → output logit

to:

> model-internal variable → model-internal variable

The key caveat: you still need to show that the latents are meaningful enough to justify this. But if you combine latent descriptions, activation contexts, dependency graphs, and intervention validation, the argument is credible.

## 3. Is Comparison Difficult Because Your Endpoint Is Different?

Yes, but it is not fatal. It just means you should not pretend the metrics are directly comparable.

The other papers evaluate against task/output endpoints because they have clear metrics:

- Circuit Tracing: graph replacement quality, graph completeness, intervention predictions, output-logit effects.
- SFC: faithfulness/completeness against a behaviour metric, plus editing outcomes.
- ADAG: description quality, simulator correlation, and intervention/steering checks.

For you, the equivalent endpoint is **seed latent activation**, not a logit. That means your evaluation should be framed as **latent-level causal faithfulness**, not task-level behavioural faithfulness.

A fair comparison would be qualitative/methodological:

- Do we produce sparse feature graphs?
- Are nodes interpretable?
- Do interventions validate the graph?
- Are graphs compact?
- Are results stable across contexts/seeds?
- Do discovered latent dependencies support downstream analysis?

You can also add optional behavioural bridges later: for some seed latents, measure whether changing the latent or its discovered upstream graph changes logits/tasks. But that should be an extension, not the core requirement.

## 4. Building Causal Chains From Star Graphs

Yes, your idea makes sense.

Current gradient outputs are mostly star-shaped:

> upstream latent A, B, C → seed latent S

A chain-building method could compose existing discoveries:

> X → A → S  
> Y → B → S  
> Z → X → A → S

Algorithmically:

1. Build circuits for many seeds.
2. Index them by seed `FeatureID`.
3. For a target seed circuit, inspect upstream nodes.
4. If an upstream node is itself a seed of another accepted circuit, splice that circuit in.
5. Repeat recursively up to `max_depth`.
6. Avoid cycles and same-layer ambiguities.
7. Re-evaluate the expanded graph against the original endpoint seed.

I’d call this something like **recursive latent dependency expansion** or **causal chain stitching**.

Important: after stitching, you need a new validation step. Otherwise it is just graph composition. The strong version asks:

> If we intervene at the earliest nodes in the stitched chain, do effects propagate through intermediate latents and recover/suppress the final seed?

That would be a meaningful next step beyond star circuits.

## 6. Can You Add Graph Replacement / Completeness Validation?

Yes, but you need to adapt it to your endpoint.

For Circuit Tracing, graph replacement asks whether the graph can stand in for the model’s local computation. For you, a seed-level version could be:

> Can the discovered circuit replace the full upstream computation well enough to reproduce the seed latent activation?

Possible metric:

- `full`: seed activation under normal model.
- `empty`: seed activation when all SAE latents in the intervention scope are zero/mean-ablated.
- `circuit_only`: keep or restore only circuit nodes, ablate non-circuit nodes in the scope.
- replacement score: `(circuit_only - empty) / (full - empty)`.

This would be much stronger than current inject/suppress evaluation. It asks whether the circuit is sufficient, not merely whether selected nodes can move the seed.

Hard part: defining “all non-circuit nodes” is huge. You probably need a bounded scope:

- same layers only;
- discovered layers only;
- top active latents only;
- upstream candidate pool only;
- SAE top-k active set per sequence.

## 7. Can You Add Faithfulness / Completeness With Complement Ablations?

Yes. This is probably easier than full graph replacement.

Use SFC-style metrics, but with latent endpoint metric `m`.

Let `m` be seed activation contrast, e.g. mean seed activation at `pos_argmax`, or pos-vs-neg difference.

Then:

**Faithfulness / sufficiency:**

> Keep circuit, ablate outside-circuit within scope. Does `m` stay high?

Approx:

```text
faithfulness = (m(circuit_only) - m(empty)) / (m(full) - m(empty))
```

**Completeness / necessity:**

> Remove circuit, keep everything else. Does `m` drop?

Approx:

```text
complement_score = (m(model_without_circuit) - m(empty)) / (m(full) - m(empty))
```

Good circuit:

- faithfulness near `1`;
- complement score near `0`;
- ablate-circuit effect large.

This would make your “these are circuits” claim much stronger.

## 8. Are Activators And Inhibitors In Other Papers?

The underlying idea exists, but your naming/framing may be distinctive.

Other papers talk about:

- positive/negative attribution;
- features increasing/decreasing logits;
- excitatory/inhibitory effects;
- promoting/suppressing outputs;
- features with positive or negative indirect effect.

But your distinction is sharper:

- **counterfactual activator**: absent on negative contexts but would push the seed up if activated.
- **counterfactual inhibitor**: present on negative contexts and suppresses the seed.
- **ablation support**: present on positive contexts and needed to maintain the seed.

That triplet is more specific than generic positive/negative attribution. I think you can claim the terminology and operational decomposition as part of your method, while acknowledging it is related to signed attribution and causal mediation.

## 9. How Does Ablation Grad Use A “Dual” Objective?

`cf_gradient` asks:

> On negative contexts, what changes would make the seed become active?

`ablation_gradient` asks the dual question:

> On positive contexts, what removals would make the seed stop being active?

In code, ablation gradient computes gradients of a positive-context seed objective, roughly seed preactivation squared, then scores active upstream latents by `activation × gradient`. If this score is positive, ablating that upstream latent is predicted to reduce the seed objective:

```27:32:X:\Projects\AIs\Turing\Publication\3 Implementation\2\src\circuit\instrument\attribution_ablation.py
Score active upstream latents by first-order benefit from ablating them.

``target_scalar`` should be a positive-context seed-loss objective, e.g.
seed pre-activation squared at the seed's peak positions. For an active
latent f with gradient g = d(loss)/df, ablating f to zero has first-order
change ``-f*g``. Therefore ``f*g > 0`` means ablation should reduce loss.
```

So “dual” means:

- counterfactual gradient: add/remove things in negative contexts to recover seed activation;
- ablation gradient: remove supports in positive contexts to suppress seed activation.

One is about **sufficiency for activation**. The other is about **necessity for maintenance**.

## 10. How Hybrid Grad Works

`hybrid_gradient` literally runs the two methods and fuses their accepted outputs.

```20:24:X:\Projects\AIs\Turing\Publication\3 Implementation\2\src\circuit\discovery\hybrid_gradient.py
class HybridGradientDiscovery(DiscoveryMethod):
    """
    Run the counterfactual and ablation gradient methods normally, then fuse any
    returned circuits into a single candidate and re-score the fused circuit.
    """
```

Flow:

1. Run `CounterfactualGradientDiscovery`.
2. Run `AblationGradientDiscovery`.
3. Keep whichever source circuits are accepted.
4. Fuse nodes by `FeatureID`.
5. Merge duplicate edges and metadata.
6. Optionally prune.
7. Re-run counterfactual faithfulness/suppression evaluation on the fused graph.
8. Accept according to `acceptance_mode`: `cf`, `suppression`, `both`, or `either`.

Conceptually, hybrid says:

> A good latent explanation may need both features that would cause the seed to appear in contrast cases and features that are necessary to sustain it in positive cases.

That is a strong framing. The weakness is that it only fuses source circuits that individually passed their acceptance gates, so two weak partial circuits cannot combine into a strong hybrid unless each survived alone.
